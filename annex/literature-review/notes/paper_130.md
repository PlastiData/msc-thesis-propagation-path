## Problem statement

Traditional reactive monitoring approaches are insufficient for managing the complexity and scale of contemporary distributed infrastructure systems. Modern distributed systems generate vast quantities of observability data that cannot be effectively processed using conventional monitoring techniques. The reactive nature of traditional monitoring means that faults are detected only after they have already begun to impact operational performance, leading to increased downtime and operational costs. Organizations require proactive fault prediction capabilities that can identify precursor signals to system failures before they cause service degradation. The challenge lies in processing real-time telemetry data from multiple sources including logs, metrics, and traces while maintaining the ability to detect subtle patterns that indicate impending failures. Current monitoring solutions lack the sophisticated pattern recognition and anomaly detection mechanisms necessary to anticipate failures in complex distributed environments.

## Method summary

The paper proposes AI-augmented infrastructure observability pipelines that integrate multiple telemetry data sources for predictive fault detection. The approach combines comprehensive data collection from logs, metrics, and distributed traces with machine learning-based pattern recognition algorithms and anomaly detection mechanisms. The framework emphasizes real-time processing capabilities to handle the volume and velocity of observability data generated by modern distributed systems. The methodology encompasses telemetry data processing pipelines that aggregate and normalize data from diverse sources, followed by AI models trained to identify patterns indicative of system failures. The implementation requires integration with existing monitoring infrastructure including distributed tracing architectures, log aggregation systems, and metrics collection frameworks. The approach employs machine learning model selection strategies tailored to specific organizational contexts and failure modes. The pipeline architecture supports proactive remediation strategies by detecting precursor signals sufficiently early to enable preventive action before failures impact service availability.

## Stated limitations

The study identifies several significant challenges and limitations in deploying AI-augmented observability pipelines. Data privacy concerns emerge as a critical issue when collecting and processing comprehensive telemetry data across distributed systems. Model interpretability requirements present challenges for operational teams who need to understand and trust the predictions generated by AI systems before taking remediation actions. Organizations face difficulties in acquiring and retaining specialized technical expertise that spans both infrastructure operations and machine learning domains. The research acknowledges that successful deployment requires careful consideration of data quality, as poor quality telemetry data can undermine model training and prediction accuracy. Integration with existing monitoring infrastructure presents technical challenges that vary across organizational contexts. The paper notes that organizational readiness for predictive maintenance approaches is a critical success factor, suggesting that cultural and operational changes may be necessary beyond purely technical implementation.

## Gaps this paper opens

The paper identifies several areas requiring further research and development. Federated learning approaches for observability data are proposed as a future direction to address data privacy concerns while enabling collaborative model training across organizations. Integration of edge computing capabilities for distributed fault detection remains an open challenge, particularly for geographically distributed systems where centralized processing may introduce latency. The abstract mentions advancement of explainability techniques but this is cut off, suggesting that model interpretability for observability predictions requires substantial additional research. The paper does not address how to establish ground truth for training and validating predictive models in production environments where labeled failure data may be scarce or biased. The relationship between different types of telemetry data and their relative importance for different failure modes is not thoroughly explored. The paper lacks concrete guidance on model selection criteria for specific system architectures or failure patterns. The temporal dynamics of failure propagation and how AI models should account for time-dependent patterns in observability data is not addressed in detail.
